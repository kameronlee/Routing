\begin{abstract}
Heat-aware walking routes must balance modeled thermal exposure against extra distance. This pilot samples Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) and shadow rasters on a Washington, DC walking network and compares four environmental objectives with shortest distance at 5--30\% detour allowances. One modeled hour and 500 origin--destination pairs produce 10,000 attempts; 6,093 of 8,000 environmental attempts return an accepted route. A reanalysis of the original records compares all five methods on 1,386 common successful pair--budget records. Mean reductions in length-weighted UTCI exceedance above $26^\circ$C range from 0.576 K for the composite objective to 0.701 K for the exceedance-focused objective. A separate, limited weight-tuning run changes 25--30\% of common accepted paths but improves this metric by only 0.044--0.050 K relative to its default. All 1,907 main-run failures log an expansion-limit error. These results characterize a capped routing implementation under one modeled snapshot. They do not establish transfer across cities, physiological benefit, or robustness to sampling and threshold choices. Code, original route records, and reanalysis scripts accompany the paper.
\end{abstract}
\keywords{pedestrian routing, UTCI, shade, detour constraints, computational benchmark}

\section{Introduction}
Two walking routes of similar length can have different shade and thermal conditions. A route that avoids exposed streets may reduce modeled heat exposure, but a long detour also adds walking. An explicit distance allowance makes this tradeoff understandable: a traveler can ask for a cooler route no more than 10\% longer than the shortest path.

This paper examines that formulation in a Washington, DC pilot. It attaches modeled UTCI and direct-shadow rasters to a pedestrian graph, then compares objectives that emphasize UTCI, shade, or a composite of environmental features. The contribution is a computational benchmark of raster-to-network integration and bounded search. Hard detour constraints, multi-label search, and environmentally enriched graphs are established ideas; the paper does not introduce a new routing algorithm.

The experiment uses 500 ordered origin--destination (OD) pairs, four detour allowances, and five methods. We compare methods on records where all five return an accepted route, examine logged failures, and reanalyze a small joint-weight experiment. Numerical results come from the archived run; corrections to the implementation are tested separately.

The distinction matters because the implementation audit found an internal distance ceiling that could exceed the final acceptance ceiling, a parallel-edge fallback issue, and a difference between two UTCI exceedance calculations. Section~\ref{sec:search} describes these behaviors. The corrected code preserves the original-network ceiling and sampled exceedance, but the numerical results here remain those recorded by the historical pipeline.

\section{Related Work}
Shade-aware routing predates this study. Vartholomaios derives least-cost paths from SOLWEIG shadows \citep{vartholomaios2023}; Da et al. use image-derived shade \citep{da2024}; and Kolaxidis et al. incorporate solar exposure into pedestrian routing \citep{kolaxidis2025}. CoolWalks studies sunlit-distance penalties and the difference between centerline and detailed pedestrian networks \citep{wolf2025}. Yang et al. update shade costs at expected arrival times \citep{yang2025}, whereas this pilot holds the environmental field fixed.

Other work uses thermal indices. Colaninno et al. combine sidewalk mobility and microclimate modeling \citep{colaninno2025}. Patel et al. rank candidate routes using SOLWEIG-GPU UTCI \citep{patel2026}. CoolPaths uses a related hard-detour formulation with length-weighted Physiological Equivalent Temperature (PET) \citep{verma2026}. Our use of UTCI, direct shadow, and additional engineering proxies changes the inputs and objectives, not the underlying constrained-routing problem. More generally, Skoumas et al. enrich route computation with contextual information about places \citep{skoumas2015}; environmental attributes are another application of that idea.

UTCI converts meteorological and radiative conditions into an equivalent temperature under standardized reference assumptions \citep{brode2012}. SOLWEIG models urban radiation and mean radiant temperature \citep{lindberg2008}. Evidence that heat influences route choice \citep{basu2024} and that pedestrians seek shade \citep{levenson2025} motivates these routing preferences. It does not calibrate this paper's weights or validate its route-level outcomes for an individual pedestrian.

\section{Routing Pipeline}
\subsection{Graph and distance budget}
Let $G_0$ be the original directed walking multigraph. For a route $P$, let $L(P)=\sum_{e\in P}l_e$, where $l_e$ is edge length in metres. For an OD pair, define the original-network shortest distance $L_0^*$ and the final ceiling
\begin{equation}
 L_0^*=\min_{P\subset G_0} L(P),\qquad D_0=(1+\delta)L_0^*.
\end{equation}
The minimization is over directed paths connecting that pair. The allowance $\delta\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20,0.30\}$ limits proportional extra distance.

To reduce feature-extraction work, a node $v$ is retained when
\begin{equation}
 d_{G_0}(o,v)+d_{G_0}(v,d)\leq D_0+50\,\mathrm{m},
\end{equation}
where $d_G$ denotes shortest-path distance on graph $G$. The pipeline then assigns raster-backed features and removes edges lacking valid UTCI samples. The resulting feature graph is $G_f$. Network processing uses OpenStreetMap, OSMnx, and NetworkX \citep{osm2026,boeing2017,hagberg2008}.

\subsection{Sampling and reported exposure}
Each source segment in an edge geometry is subdivided to a maximum spacing of 20 m, retaining endpoints. Coordinates are transformed into each raster's coordinate reference system before sampling. Non-finite and NoData samples are omitted. Edges without UTCI samples are removed; edges without direct-shadow samples use the surrogate in Appendix~\ref{app:features}.

For UTCI samples $u_{ei}$ on edge $e$, define
\begin{align}
 \bar u_e&=\frac{1}{n_e^u}\sum_i u_{ei},\qquad u_e^{\max}=\max_i u_{ei},\\
 h_e&=\frac{1}{n_e^u}\sum_i\max(0,u_{ei}-\tau),\quad \tau=26^\circ\mathrm C.
\end{align}
For valid shadow samples $b_{ej}$, the direct-shade fraction is $s_e^{\rm dir}=(n_e^b)^{-1}\sum_j\mathbf1[b_{ej}\leq0.5]$; the raster convention is zero for shade and one for sun. Except for hybrid azimuth, each method uses this value when present and the surrogate $s_e^{\rm az}$ otherwise. The hybrid-azimuth method uses $s_e^{\rm az}$ everywhere.

The $26^\circ$C reference is the conventional UTCI boundary between no thermal stress and moderate heat stress \citep{eeautci}. Its use here is an engineering reference, not a personalized safety threshold. The 20 m interval reduces sampling work on nominal 1 m rasters. It can miss short shaded segments; no convergence study establishes its accuracy.

Reported route quantities are length-weighted edge summaries:
\begin{align}
 H(P)&=\frac{\sum_e l_eh_e}{L(P)},&\bar U(P)&=\frac{\sum_e l_e\bar u_e}{L(P)},\\
 S(P)&=\frac{\sum_e l_es_e}{L(P)},&U^{\max}(P)&=\max_e u_e^{\max}.
\end{align}
$H$ is mean positive UTCI exceedance, expressed in kelvins; $\bar U$ and $U^{\max}$ are UTCI values in degrees Celsius. $S$ is a shade fraction. Because sampling restarts at segment boundaries, these quantities are numerical proxies rather than uniform line integrals. In particular, $H$ is not a time-integrated dose: a longer route with lower $H$ can still entail more total exposure.

\subsection{Objectives and method variants}
The composite cost is our engineering construction. It combines distance and time with thermal, scenic, traffic, and slope penalties; Appendix~\ref{app:cost} specifies the terms and normalizations. All are nonnegative. The thermal term is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thermal}
 \phi_e=\frac{[\bar u_e-\tau]_+}{8\,\mathrm K}+\frac{[u_e^{\max}-\tau]_+}{10\,\mathrm K}+\frac{h_e}{6\,\mathrm K}+0.45(1-s_e),
\end{equation}
where $[x]_+=\max(0,x)$. Its correlated UTCI and shade components can count related radiative information more than once. Neither its coefficients nor the contextual proxies are fitted to route-choice or physiological observations.

Table~\ref{tab:methods} treats the environmental methods as objective variants of one implementation. Distance and time remain active in all four. The focused methods are therefore not pure single-feature optimizations. For reporting, selected routes are evaluated with full features; the hybrid shade outcome remains a surrogate and is not comparable with direct shade.

\subsection{Search, acceptance, and implementation audit}\label{sec:search}
In the historical formulation, the internal ceiling is $D_f=(1+\delta)L_f^*$, where $L_f^*$ is shortest distance on $G_f$. Removing edges cannot shorten a shortest path, so $L_f^*\geq L_0^*$ and $D_f\geq D_0$. The internal search can therefore admit a candidate that fails the final original-network check.

The solver first computes an unconstrained minimum-cost path and returns it if it meets $D_f$. Otherwise it explores cost--distance labels. At a node, a label dominates another if it has no greater length and no greater cost. Labels are also pruned when accumulated length plus shortest remaining distance exceeds $D_f$. Paths may not repeat nodes.

The dominance registry retains at most eight labels per node, ordered by cost then length. A newly queued label can survive even when omitted from that registry, so this is not a bound on the queue size. The label loop stops after 20,000 queue removals or 1.5 s, with timing beginning after the initial cost-path calculation. These caps remove an optimality guarantee and do not bound end-to-end latency.

The inspected pre-revision fallback obtains a length-shortest node sequence but chooses the lowest-cost parallel edge between consecutive nodes. That can produce a longer edge sequence than the shortest one. It also projects $h_e$ to $[\bar u_e-\tau]_+$ during objective selection; in general this differs from mean positive sampled exceedance. The reporting metric above retains the sampled definition. The historical execution commit was not recorded, so the audit cannot establish how much these discrepancies affected that run.

The corrected implementation propagates $D_0$ throughout search, uses shortest parallel edges for shortest-route fallback, and preserves sampled exceedance. It also records path source, cap termination, and rejection type. These fixes require a fresh real-data experiment. The present analysis keeps the historical outcomes separate. An accepted historical route passes the final distance check, but its unmarked source may be the initial cost path, label search, or fallback.

The study represents Washington, DC on June 19, 2026 at an intended civil time of 14:00. Walking is relevant in this setting: Sustainable DC 2.0 reports a 13.3\% walking commute share in 2016 \citep{sustainabledc}. This historical district-wide statistic does not make the sampled OD pairs representative of actual trips.

The fields were not independently validated here. The retained provenance does not pin the forecast retrieval time, automatically selected forecast model, or environmental-model release. The routing timestamp has no UTC offset; exact recovery of the hybrid solar-position calculation would require the historical host offset.

The generator uses the largest weakly connected graph component, then retains endpoints with finite UTCI and shadow raster values. With seed 42, it samples 500 distinct ordered node pairs whose shortest network distances lie between 400 and 3,000 m. The recovered distances range from 412.9 to 2,998.9 m. Each pair is evaluated with five methods at four allowances, giving 10,000 attempts. \subsection{Matched comparison and uncertainty}\label{sec:matched-analysis}
Let $P^0_{i\delta}$ be the matched shortest route and $P^m_{i\delta}$ the route returned by method $m$. Positive improvements are
\begin{equation}
 \Delta H_{i\delta m}=H(P^0_{i\delta})-H(P^m_{i\delta}),
\end{equation}
with analogous $\Delta\bar U$; shade change reverses the order. Extra distance is $100(L(P^m_{i\delta})/L_0^*-1)$.

At each allowance, the primary comparison uses the intersection $\mathcal C_\delta$ of successful records for all five methods. Matching includes study, configuration hash, run identifier, raster timestamp, OD identifier, and allowance; duplicates are rejected. The common set contains 389, 318, 309, and 370 OD pairs at 5\%, 10\%, 20\%, and 30\%, respectively. Pooled means weight the resulting 1,386 pair--budget records equally and cover 459 distinct OD pairs. They describe this selected subset, not every attempted trip.

For a paired method contrast, we compute the mean difference in recorded $H$ on the same common records. Percentile intervals use 2,000 bootstrap resamples of whole OD pairs, retaining each pair's available allowances, with seed 42. These exploratory 95\% intervals are not adjusted for multiple comparisons. They summarize variability within this one modeled snapshot and do not account for microclimate error or selection of the successful subset.

\subsection{Weight-tuning reanalysis}
The archive also contains 800 attempts from the first 100 serialized OD pairs at 10\% and 20\% allowances. Four composite configurations jointly change several weights (Table~\ref{tab:weights}). All other recorded study identifiers match the main run. The four variants share 142 successful pair--budget records from 81 pairs; all recorded successful paths meet the original distance ceiling. We compare variants on this intersection and score their recorded $H$ under the same threshold and field. Path change means a different ordered edge sequence.

This is a limited joint-weight exploration, not an independent validation set or a one-factor sensitivity study. The archive does not establish whether the alternatives were selected before inspecting results. Threshold, spacing, and corrected-code sensitivity runs remain outstanding. We exclude the separate September uncertainty experiments because they use a different OD cohort, network, fields, threshold, and routing design.

All 1,907 failed records contain the same message: the search exceeded 20,000 expansions before finding a route within the detour budget. This identifies the logged termination category, not a proof of geographic infeasibility or the full causal role of coverage and budget handling. Expansion counters and fallback-source flags are blank in the historical records.

Every original-graph OD pair has its own shortest path within $D_0$. Consequently, failure to return an environmental route does not mean that the neighborhood has no walkable route or no cooler alternative. Feature removal can eliminate the baseline, and the capped procedure may stop before finding another feasible path. This acceptance level is a material implementation limitation, not evidence that one quarter of trips are geographically infeasible.

Positive averages also conceal adverse outcomes. In the full method-specific accepted sets, the 10th-percentile $\Delta H$ is negative for every environmental variant. Median reduction in maximum sampled UTCI is zero for each variant. Thus lower mean exposure does not imply improvement on every route or removal of its hottest segment. The hybrid's apparent shade improvement is especially unsuitable for ranking because its reporting field differs; direct-shadow methods can also substitute the surrogate on edges with missing shadow samples, with no historical substitution count.

\subsection{Detour allowances}
For the composite's method-specific accepted records, mean $\Delta H$ rises from 0.411 K at 5\% allowance to 0.756 K at 30\%, while mean extra distance rises from 3.37\% to 14.50\%. Acceptance is non-monotonic: 401, 349, 376, and 428 of 500 attempts succeed at the four allowances. These changing subsets do not identify a within-pair dose--response curve.

On a fixed graph, the set of paths meeting the final distance inequality grows with the allowance. The historical experiment nevertheless rebuilds its corridor, uses an internal feature-graph ceiling, and applies search caps. Its non-monotonic acceptance therefore reflects the implementation rather than a failure of that nesting property. A corrected rerun and cap sweep are needed to distinguish the contributions of those stages.

\subsection{Sensitivity to joint weight changes}

OD-cluster intervals for the additional reductions are 0.017--0.085 K, 0.020--0.088 K, and 0.023--0.091 K, respectively.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The common-set reanalysis supports a narrow result: under this modeled field, focused thermal objectives reduce recorded mean UTCI exceedance more than the default composite on OD--budget records where every method succeeds. The composite's mean distance overhead is smaller. The analysis does not establish a universally best route, because the objectives encode different preferences and the successful subset excludes difficult attempts.

The failure log strengthens the diagnosis of the acceptance problem. The recorded failure category is computational, and a public-facing router should distinguish a capped search from a proof that no feasible path exists. It should also identify shortest-route fallback explicitly. Exact or high-cap comparisons on smaller graphs would quantify approximation error; the current benchmark has no optimality-gap measurement.

The spatial and temporal scope remains limited to one modeled DC hour. The experiment cannot establish transfer across cities, seasons, neighborhoods, or times of day. A fixed raster also ignores changes during a walk. The original sampling targets covered graph nodes, not observed pedestrian demand, so its outcomes should not be interpreted as population benefits.

Fine raster resolution does not remove representation error. Street centerlines may lie in sun while a sidewalk is shaded, or the reverse. Source layers have different vintages, the traffic join uses a nearest-midpoint proxy in geographic coordinates without a maximum distance, and nominal canopy does not guarantee shade at the pedestrian's position. The hybrid shade value mixes geometry and cool UTCI samples; it is not a substitute for independently validated direct shadow.

The composite is uncalibrated. Thermal terms overlap, scenic and traffic scores are engineering proxies, and the uncertainty input is zero in this experiment. The limited tuning analysis adds evidence about three joint weight alternatives, but not about individual coefficients, threshold choice, or sampling convergence. Those questions require rerouting fixed OD pairs with controlled perturbations and scoring every selected path on a common reference field. The revised code supplies the workflow for those runs.

This pilot has no field transects, participant study, or health measurements. Lower length-weighted $H$ does not establish reduced cumulative exposure, comfort, safety, or clinical benefit. Deployment would require independent environmental validation, sidewalk-level networks, and traveler-specific constraints.

\section{Conclusion}
This pilot connects UTCI and shadow rasters to a walking network under explicit detour allowances. Reanalysis of the original route records resolves the unequal-subset comparison and identifies the logged search-limit failures. On 1,386 common successful OD--budget records, mean exceedance reductions are 0.576--0.701 K with 8.32--9.30\% extra distance. A small joint-weight experiment changes a quarter to a third of its common paths while modestly changing the exposure summary. The remaining priorities are a corrected real-data rerun, threshold and sampling checks, and evaluation across additional snapshots.

\section{Engineering Feature Definitions}\label{app:features}
Let $\operatorname{clip}(x)=\min(1,\max(0,x))$. Canopy and land cover are sampled on the densified centerline; building height is additionally sampled at lateral offsets of one-half and one estimated street width.

Let $c_e$ be the fraction of canopy samples at least 2 m high; $k_e$ the mean canopy height divided by 12 m and clipped; $v_e$ the larger of canopy- and land-cover-derived greenery fractions; and $w_e$ the water fraction. The scenic proxy is
\begin{equation}
 g_e=\operatorname{clip}(0.60c_e+0.25k_e+0.10v_e+0.05w_e).
\end{equation}
For nearest-midpoint AADT $a_e$ and the traffic layer's maximum $a_{\max}$, the traffic proxy is $n_e=\log_{10}(a_e+1)/\log_{10}(a_{\max}+1)$. This is not a decibel scale.

For solar altitude $\alpha$, azimuth $\theta$, bearing $\beta_e$, and estimated street width $W_e$, define
\begin{equation}
 q_e(z)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{z|\sin(\theta-\beta_e)|}{W_e\max(\tan\alpha,10^{-6})}\right).
\end{equation}
If explicit width is missing, $W_e=\max(4,3.2p_e)$ m for $p_e$ lanes, or 10 m if lane count is also missing. With mean and maximum canopy/building heights, use $z_e^c=\max(\bar z_e^c,0.5z_e^{c,\max})$ and $z_e^b=\max(\bar z_e^b,0.6z_e^{b,\max})$. The geometry shade proxy is
\begin{equation}
s_e^{\rm geom}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\max\{c_e,q_e(z_e^b),c_eq_e(z_e^c)\}\right),
\end{equation}
set to one below the horizon. For local fractional hour $t$,
\begin{align}
 \tau_c(t)&=[25.5+3.2\cos(2\pi(t-14)/24)]^\circ\mathrm C,\\
 s_e^{\rm cool}&=(n_e^u)^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf1[u_{ei}\leq\tau_c(t)],\\
 s_e^{\rm az}&=\max(s_e^{\rm cool},s_e^{\rm geom}).
\end{align}
At $t=14$, the cool threshold is $28.7^\circ$C. These coefficients define a hybrid surrogate, not a fitted shadow model.

\section{Composite Cost}\label{app:cost}
Let signed elevation change be $\Delta z_e$, grade $m_e=\Delta z_e/l_e$, and $r_e=10|m_e|+[\Delta z_e]_+/(25\,\mathrm m)$. Edge time is $t_e$, estimated as $l_e/(1.35\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})$ when unavailable. With $\phi_e$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:thermal}, the default cost is
\begin{align}
 A_e&=0.15\frac{l_e}{1000\,\mathrm m}+0.20\frac{t_e}{600\,\mathrm s},\\
 C_e&=A_e+\frac{\max(l_e,1\,\mathrm m)}{100\,\mathrm m}\bigl[\phi_e+0.45(1-g_e)^2\nonumber\\
 &\hspace{31mm}+0.45n_e^2+0.35r_e+0.15\sigma_e\bigr],\\
 C(P)&=\sum_{e\in P}C_e.
\end{align}
All ratios are dimensionless. $\sigma_e=0$ throughout this experiment. Table~\ref{tab:weights} changes the corresponding coefficients and the shade multiplier. The 1 m floor prevents a zero environmental scale on a malformed zero-length edge. These formulas specify the intended engineering score; Section~\ref{sec:search} describes the audited projection discrepancy and the uncertainty about the historical execution version.
